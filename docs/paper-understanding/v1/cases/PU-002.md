## 1. Problem Origin｜问题为何产生？

**Existing knowledge:** Urban centres typically concentrate jobs and offer strong potential accessibility, while changes in housing markets and residential composition redistribute access to such places.

**Tension:** High average accessibility does not imply that residents from different occupational groups have comparable access to opportunities. Where professionals can afford to live and how lower-paid workers travel may alter who benefits from a compact urban form. *(Introduction and literature review, Sections 1–2.)*

## 2. Scientific Problem Reframing｜科学问题如何变化？

- **General question:** How many jobs can be reached from each location within a specified travel time?
- **Study focus:** Which occupational classes occupy highly accessible locations, by which modes can they reach jobs, and how do these patterns relate to residential and housing structure in metropolitan London?
- **Core shift:** From average spatial accessibility to the **distribution of opportunity across social groups and feasible transport modes**.

**Boundary:** Transport equity and class-specific accessibility were already established research areas; this study did not invent group-differentiated accessibility. Its contribution is a specific empirical examination in metropolitan London with occupational, transport-mode and housing dimensions.

## 3. Prior Knowledge & Gap｜已有认识与知识缺口

Accessibility metrics and compact-city planning have often focused on spatial efficiency and available destinations. Other scholarship has examined unequal mobility, housing markets and gentrification. This study connects these literatures to ask whether inner-city accessibility advantages accrue unequally to occupational classes. *(Sections 1, 2.1–2.2.)*

**Not established:** That earlier studies universally ignored equity, or that gentrification is the proven sole cause of differences.

## 4. Scientific Reasoning｜从问题形成经验假设

If high-accessibility neighbourhoods are more expensive, occupational classes with different resources may occupy different residential locations; access differences can depend on how transport modes distribute connectivity.

**Competing explanatory families:** Occupational residential sorting; public transport and pedestrian network geography; employment-centre distribution; social housing buffering exclusion.

**Key testable question:** Do professional classes have systematically higher transit, bus and walking job accessibility than lower-paid classes, despite comparatively smaller inequality by car? These are observed *associations*, not causally isolated pathways.

## 5. Method Necessity｜方法为什么必要？

The analysis uses the London metropolitan region beyond the inner-city administrative boundary, occupational-class population information, jobs and multimodal transport-network travel times to calculate cumulative employment opportunities under time thresholds. The study also discusses occupation distribution changes and housing tenure patterns. *(Methods, Section 3.)*

**Why method follows question:** Aggregate area-level means conceal class distribution; single-mode accessibility conceals dependence on less expensive modes; a truncated central-city geography conceals metropolitan periphery conditions.

**Measurement caveat:** Jobs reachable within time is **potential accessibility**. It does not prove that individuals secured those jobs, matched qualifications, faced affordable fares, or could travel at every time of day.

## 6. Evidence Chain｜主要结果支持什么？

The study reports relatively smaller differences in car accessibility, alongside clearer occupational-class inequalities for public transport, bus and walking; professional groups tend to fare better. Accounting for the wider metropolitan area affects the inequality picture; housing-tenure and occupational residential patterns provide context. *(Results and discussion, Sections 4–6; exact tables/figures to be verified.)*

**High-level conclusion supported:** A metropolitan area may have high aggregate connectivity while employment opportunities are unequally reachable across classes.

**Not a demonstrated causal conclusion:** The 2011 cross-sectional accessibility patterns do not by themselves identify a causal effect of gentrification or tenure on individual jobs outcomes.

## 7. Contribution & Limits｜科研贡献与局限

1. Measures the distributional consequences of employment accessibility by occupation across metropolitan London.
2. Shows why evaluation by different modes matters for equity.
3. Connects transport opportunity with residential/housing context, posing questions beyond pure network efficiency.

**Limits:** Main accessibility comparison is cross-sectional, based on 2011 data; generalized travel-time thresholds, omission or incomplete representation of monetary costs, job competition and skills matching, peak/off-peak variation and residential selection constrain interpretation. Verify which of these are explicitly stated by the authors versus analyst inference.

## 8. Transferable Insight｜可迁移的判断

**Pattern: Distributional Question Reframing.** When a service or policy is praised for high coverage, efficiency or average accessibility, ask *who receives the benefit, under what constraints, and whether the aggregate result conceals systematically different outcomes*.

**Applicability conditions:** A plausible distribution mechanism, distinguishable beneficiary groups, usable data for fair comparisons, and an explanation why changing the unit of evaluation matters to the central question.

**Failure pattern:** Adding an occupational-class variable to an accessibility regression, without articulating a different substantive question, does not automatically amount to a scientific contribution.

**Positive transfer:** Ask whether a citywide gain in transport reachability improves realistic job access for residents relying on bus or walking, rather than assuming equal benefit.

**Counterfactual test:** If the accessibility algorithm is substituted with an equally valid tool, does the distributional question still matter? Can the same data distinguish positional disparities from causal displacement effects? If not, avoid causal claims.
